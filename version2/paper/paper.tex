\title{The Descriptor is Not a Lens: \\
       Behavioral Descriptors as Implicit Selection Pressure
       in Quality-Diversity Procedural Content Generation}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Submission}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Quality-diversity (QD) algorithms have become a default tool for
procedural content generation (PCG): they return not a single best
artefact but a whole archive of high-fit niches, indexed by a
hand-chosen \emph{behavioral descriptor}. The descriptor is usually
treated as a passive lens onto the design space --- a way to ask the
search to be diverse. We argue and demonstrate that this view is
wrong. A behavioral descriptor is an \emph{active selection
pressure}: it determines which mutated offspring survive to become
parents, and the probability of survival depends on properties of
the genome that may not appear in the fitness function at all.

We show this empirically in two PCG domains --- Sokoban puzzle
generation and tile-based platformer level generation --- under a
controlled cross-condition design that holds the genome, mutation
operators, fitness function, and compute budget fixed and varies only
the descriptor. Across five conditions and twenty replicates per
condition per domain (200 runs, $\approx 1.05$M evaluations), we
find that (i) descriptor choice can determine whether MAP-Elites
even beats random search: in the platformer, only one of four
descriptor variants does, and one is significantly \emph{worse}
than random ($p_\text{adj}<10^{-3}$, Cliff's $\delta=-0.80$);
(ii) in Sokoban, the ``cheap'' structural descriptor is
$2.2\times$ slower than the gameplay descriptor because it pushes
selection toward boards with a $20\%$ infeasibility rate (vs
$13\%$ for the gameplay descriptor) --- the implicit selection
pressure shows up not as harder \emph{solvable} boards but as more
\emph{attempted-then-timed-out} ones;
(iii) the same descriptor causes no wall-clock overhead at all in
the platformer because the platformer's A* solver is uniformly
fast and infeasibility rates are uniform across conditions ---
the mechanism's wall-clock manifestation is therefore
domain-dependent;
(iv) the rank-ordering of descriptor families is not preserved
across domains, contradicting the implicit assumption that
``structural'' descriptors are universally cheap or universally
informative. We close with a practical decision guide for
descriptor selection in QD-PCG and a replication package.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Quality-diversity (QD) algorithms~\cite{lehman2011novelty,
mouret2015illuminating, cully2017qd, pugh2016quality} return not a
single best artefact but an archive of locally-best artefacts indexed
by a hand-chosen \emph{behavioral descriptor}. For procedural content
generation (PCG), this is precisely what designers want: an explicit
library of distinct levels, puzzles, or scenes that vary along an
axis of editorial interest~\cite{gravina2019pcgsurvey,
khalifa2018talakat, fontaine2021illuminating, earle2022illuminating}.

In practice, the descriptor is treated almost as part of the problem
statement --- a passive lens through which the designer asks the
search to vary. ``Use the level's width and number of platforms,''
the convention goes, ``and MAP-Elites will give you a varied set of
platformer levels.'' This view is implicit in nearly every QD-PCG
paper and is rarely empirically interrogated.

This paper argues that the view is incorrect, and substantiates the
argument across two PCG domains.

\paragraph{Thesis} A behavioral descriptor in MAP-Elites is not a
passive lens; it is an \emph{implicit selection pressure}. Two
candidate artefacts produced by the same mutation step receive
different survival probabilities depending on which descriptor cell
they fall into, on whether that cell is already occupied, and on
which other artefacts later compete with them in the same cell. The
designer typically reasons about the descriptor as an axis of
\emph{display} (``I want to see levels of varying length''); we show
that it is also an axis of \emph{selection} (``I am, possibly
without realising it, asking my search to prefer levels that are
hard for $A^{*}$ to plan in'').

\paragraph{What this paper does} We hold every other component of the
search fixed --- genome encoding, mutation operators, fitness
function, compute budget, evaluator --- and vary only the
descriptor. We do this in two PCG domains: 8$\times$8 Sokoban puzzle
generation and 10$\times$24 tile-based platformer level generation.
Both domains share the same MAP-Elites driver and the same family of
five conditions: a random-search baseline plus four MAP-Elites
variants using (i) a purely structural descriptor (\textsf{STR}),
(ii) a gameplay descriptor derived from the solver's plan
(\textsf{PLAY}), (iii) a solver-effort descriptor (\textsf{SKILL}),
and (iv) a descriptor that matches the held-out projection used to
score archives (\textsf{COMMON}, an upper-bound reference). Each
condition is run for 20 random seeds.

\paragraph{Findings}
\emph{(F1, replication --- with a surprise.)} In Sokoban every
MAP-Elites variant strictly outperforms random search on coverage
and QD-score: \textsf{STR}, \textsf{SKILL}, and \textsf{COMMON}
attain $|\delta|=1.0$ (no overlap across seeds), \textsf{PLAY}
attains $|\delta|=0.998$ (one near-tied pair), all
$p_\text{adj}\!<\!10^{-6}$.
In the platformer, however, the picture is messier: only
\textsf{COMMON} significantly beats \textsf{RAND} ($\delta\!=\!+0.82$,
$p_\text{adj}\!<\!10^{-4}$); \textsf{STR} and \textsf{SKILL} are
statistically indistinguishable from \textsf{RAND} on coverage
($p_\text{adj}\!=\!0.77$ and $0.47$ respectively); and
\textsf{PLAY} is significantly \emph{worse} than \textsf{RAND}
($\delta\!=\!-0.80$, $p_\text{adj}\!<\!10^{-3}$). With the wrong descriptor, MAP-Elites
can actively underperform a baseline that uses none --- a direct
demonstration of the implicit-pressure thesis.
\emph{(F2, descriptor matters.)} The descriptor choice substantially
reshapes the projected archive: the gap between the best
(\textsf{COMMON}, $37.3\!\pm\!1.7$ cells) and worst (\textsf{PLAY},
$31.3\!\pm\!2.5$) MAP-Elites condition in Sokoban is $16\%$ of
\textsf{COMMON}'s mean and statistically significant
($p_\text{adj}\!\approx\!10^{-6}$, $\delta\!=\!+0.95$, large).
The same gap is even larger in the platformer at $22\%$
($\delta\!=\!+0.95$).
\emph{(F3, hidden hardness selection in Sokoban.)} \textsf{STR}'s
descriptor selects toward dense-wall elites; mutating from those
elites produces a $20.0\%$ unsolvable-but-attempted rate vs $12.9\%$
for \textsf{PLAY}. Each timeout exhausts the full $8000$-node
budget, so the additional failure work alone accounts for most of
the $2.2\times$ wall-clock overhead of \textsf{STR} over
\textsf{PLAY} ($81.8\!\pm\!11.4$\,s vs $37.3\!\pm\!11.6$\,s,
$p_\text{adj}\!<\!10^{-6}$, $\delta\!=\!-0.99$). The same mechanism
is absent in the platformer (MAP-Elites timeout rates $5{-}6\%$
across all conditions; wall-clock differences within a factor of
$1.4$).
\emph{(F4, no universal ordering among indirect descriptors.)}
\textsf{PLAY} is the worst MAP-Elites variant in both domains, but
\textsf{STR} and \textsf{SKILL} swap rank: \textsf{SKILL} narrowly
wins in Sokoban ($33.8$ vs $32.4$ for \textsf{STR}); \textsf{STR}
narrowly wins in the platformer ($39.9$ vs $38.9$ for
\textsf{SKILL}). The popular intuition that ``structural''
descriptors are universally cheap or that ``gameplay'' descriptors
are universally informative does not survive a controlled
comparison.

\paragraph{Contributions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A reframing of behavioral descriptors as implicit selection
        pressures, with concrete falsifiable predictions and an
        evaluation protocol (\S\ref{sec:method}).
  \item A two-domain, five-condition, $20$-seed empirical study with
        properly multiplicity-corrected effect sizes
        (\S\ref{sec:experiments}--\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A mechanism analysis showing that the descriptor's
        correlation with solver hardness predicts the magnitude of
        the implicit selection bias (\S\ref{sec:mechanism}).
  \item A practical descriptor-choice decision guide
        (\S\ref{sec:discussion}) and a full replication package.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Quality diversity}

The conceptual root of QD is Lehman and Stanley's novelty
search~\cite{lehman2011novelty}, which discards the fitness signal
entirely in favour of behavioural novelty as the selection
pressure. Novelty search demonstrated that ``where you look'' can be
more important than ``what you look for'' --- a result that prefigures
the central claim of this paper. Mouret and Clune's
MAP-Elites~\cite{mouret2015illuminating} integrated novelty and
fitness into a single algorithm by retaining the best individual per
cell of a discretised behaviour space. Pugh et al.\
\cite{pugh2016quality} provided the first systematic taxonomy of
quality-diversity, and Cully and Demiris~\cite{cully2017qd} gave a
modular framework that makes the role of the descriptor explicit.

\subsection{Variants of MAP-Elites}

Several extensions of MAP-Elites address its archive-management
mechanics. CMA-ME~\cite{fontaine2020cma} replaces the uniform-random
parent selection with a CMA-ES-style emitter that adapts the search
distribution to the active frontier. CMA-MAE~\cite{fontaine2023cma}
generalises CMA-ME by annealing a soft archive that tracks both
discovery and improvement rates. CVT-MAP-Elites
\cite{vassiliades2017cvtmap} replaces the rectangular grid with a
centroidal Voronoi tessellation that scales to high-dimensional
descriptor spaces. DSAGE~\cite{bhatt2022dsage} uses surrogate
models to extrapolate the archive at lower evaluation cost.
\emph{None of these variants change the descriptor itself:} all
inherit whatever descriptor the user provides. The phenomenon we
investigate --- implicit selection through descriptor choice --- is
therefore shared by every method in this family.

\subsection{Adaptive and learned descriptors}

A small but growing line of work learns the behavioural descriptor
rather than hand-designing it. AURORA~\cite{cully2019autonomous}
learns a low-dimensional behavioural embedding through an
autoencoder on observed sensory traces, with the avowed goal of
making the descriptor problem-agnostic.
AURORA-XCon~\cite{grillotti2022unsupervised} adds a contrastive
constraint so the learned descriptor remains diverse under archive
evolution. Paolo et al.~\cite{paolo2020unsupervised} explore
reachable outcome spaces in robot locomotion. These approaches
implicitly acknowledge the problem we make explicit: hand-chosen
descriptors are a substantial design decision whose effects are
poorly characterised. Our study is complementary --- it documents
\emph{how much} a descriptor choice matters when it is, as usual,
hand-chosen.

\subsection{PCG via QD}

The use of QD for PCG was popularised by
Talakat~\cite{khalifa2018talakat} (bullet-hell), Earle et al.\
\cite{earle2022illuminating} (neural cellular automata for diverse
level generation), and Fontaine et al.~\cite{fontaine2021illuminating}
(latent-space search over GAN-generated Mario scenes). The
Gravina et al.\ survey~\cite{gravina2019pcgsurvey} reviews QD-PCG
through 2019. The PCG-via-machine-learning
literature~\cite{summerville2018procedural} and the PCGRL family
\cite{khalifa2020pcgrl} sit alongside QD-PCG as alternative
paradigms but do not replace its niche-finding capability.
Throughout this literature, descriptors are chosen \emph{ad hoc}
(``number of enemies and length of solution path,'' or ``mean
brightness and number of bricks''). To our knowledge, the present
paper is the first systematic head-to-head comparison of descriptor
\emph{families} under a controlled protocol across multiple PCG
domains.

\subsection{Sokoban and platformer level generation}

Sokoban is PSPACE-complete~\cite{culberson1999sokoban} and has been
a perennial PCG target~\cite{taylor2011sokoban, murofushi2018sokoban,
kowalski2023sokoban}. Domain-specific solvers~\cite{junghanns2001sokoban}
make evaluation tractable enough for evolutionary search but slow
enough that the descriptor's implicit hardness pressure is
economically meaningful. Mario-style platformer generation has been
studied with evolutionary search~\cite{shaker2010mario},
GANs~\cite{volz2018evolving}, Markov chains~\cite{snodgrass2017markov},
and PCGRL~\cite{khalifa2020pcgrl}. The tile-grid platformer we use
here is a deliberate simplification chosen so that the
\emph{descriptor families} are directly comparable to Sokoban's, not
to compete with the state of the art in Mario generation.

\subsection{Hidden selection in evolutionary computation}

The general observation that evolutionary algorithms can be steered
by features absent from the fitness function is folklore in the EC
community. Lehman's collection of evolutionary
``surprises''~\cite{lehman2020surprising} catalogues many such
cases. Constrained novelty search~\cite{liapis2015constrained} and
designer modelling~\cite{liapis2014designer, holmgard2014personas}
explicitly engineer additional selection pressures. The contribution
of the present paper is to show that, in QD-PCG specifically, the
behavioral descriptor is one such pressure even when --- especially
when --- the practitioner believes it to be merely an axis of
display.

\section{Method: Descriptors as Implicit Selection Pressure}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Formalisation}

Let $\mathcal{G}$ be the genome space (e.g.\ tile-grid levels), let
$f:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}$ be the fitness function, and
let $d:\mathcal{G}\to\mathcal{C}$ map each genome into one of finitely
many descriptor cells $\mathcal{C}$. MAP-Elites maintains an archive
$A: \mathcal{C}\to \mathcal{G}$ that stores the best-fitness genome
discovered so far in each cell. Each generation, the algorithm picks
a parent $g_\text{par}$ uniformly from $A$, mutates to obtain
$g_\text{ch}$, and inserts $g_\text{ch}$ into $A[d(g_\text{ch})]$ iff
$f(g_\text{ch}) > f(A[d(g_\text{ch})])$.

The \emph{explicit} selection pressure on a child $g_\text{ch}$ is
$f$. The \emph{implicit} selection pressure is the cell map $d$:
$g_\text{ch}$ survives if and only if its descriptor cell happens to
be empty or contain a worse-fitness occupant. For two children with
identical fitness, descriptor cell occupancy determines survival.

Two descriptors $d_1, d_2$ that induce the same set of cells but
assign genomes differently to them will produce different archives
even with the same $f$ and the same mutation operator. We make this
quantitative: let $p_d(g)$ denote the probability that a genome $g$
appears as a cell-winner in the eventual archive under descriptor
$d$, and let $X(g)$ be any property of $g$ (e.g.\ the number of A*
nodes the solver expands). Then
\[
   \mathbb{E}[X \mid \text{retained under } d_1] \neq
   \mathbb{E}[X \mid \text{retained under } d_2]
\]
in general, even when $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \text{evaluated}]$ is the
same for both. This expectation gap is what we call the
\emph{retention bias} of $d$, and it is the empirical observable we
target in \S\ref{sec:mechanism}.

\subsection{Descriptor families}

A descriptor family is the analogue of a hyperparameter axis in
standard ML. We compare four:

\begin{description}
  \item[\textsf{RAND}] Random search; no MAP-Elites archive. The
        baseline.
  \item[\textsf{STR}] \emph{Structural}: descriptor coordinates are
        computable from the genome alone, without running the
        solver. Cheap; intuitive; the default in many QD-PCG papers.
  \item[\textsf{PLAY}] \emph{Gameplay}: coordinates derived from
        properties of the solver's plan (length, jumps, pushes).
        Captures what a player would experience.
  \item[\textsf{SKILL}] \emph{Solver effort}: coordinates from
        solver-internal quantities (nodes expanded, depth reached).
        Captures how hard the level is for the agent.
  \item[\textsf{COMMON}] A reference condition: ME uses the
        \emph{same} descriptor it is evaluated against
        (\#boxes$\times$plan-length in Sokoban; \#pits$\times$plan-length
        in the platformer). By construction this gives an upper
        bound on common-archive coverage.
\end{description}

All MAP-Elites variants share the same fitness, the same mutation
operators, and the same initialisation. Only $d$ varies.

\subsection{Cross-condition evaluation: the common projection}

Different descriptors give archives of different shapes and over
different axes. To compare them fairly, every archive is projected
onto a shared \emph{common descriptor} that names the design
properties a downstream designer would care about. In Sokoban the
common descriptor is $(\text{n\_boxes}, \text{plan\_length})$ on a
$4\times 10$ grid; in the platformer it is $(\text{n\_pits},
\text{plan\_length})$ on an $8\times 10$ grid. The common-archive's
coverage and QD-score are the headline metrics in this paper. The
common projection has a long-standing tradition in QD
benchmarking~\cite{pugh2016quality, chatzilygeroudis2021qd}.

\section{Domains}
\label{sec:domains}

\subsection{Sokoban (\textbf{D1})}

Sokoban levels are $8\times 8$ grids with walls, goals, a player, and
1--3 boxes. The genome is the direct grid encoding; mutations toggle
single tiles, move boxes/goals/player, or add/remove box--goal
pairs. The solver is A* over (player, box-set) states with a
deadlock-pruned Manhattan-distance heuristic
\cite{junghanns2001sokoban}; the node budget is $8000$. Fitness
rewards solvability, plan length, push count, and a triangular
structural bonus peaked at wall density $0.2$. Sokoban is
PSPACE-complete \cite{culberson1999sokoban}; even with deadlock
pruning, node expansion varies over several orders of magnitude
across the design space, which is what gives \textsf{STR}'s implicit
hardness bias bite.

\subsection{Tile-based platformer (\textbf{D2})}

Platformer levels are $10\times 24$ tile grids of empty / solid /
spike tiles. The player starts at the top of column~0's ground stack
and must reach any standing position in column $23$. Physics is a
standing-to-standing graph: from each standing position the agent can
walk, walk-off-a-ledge, or jump $(dx,dy)$ with $dx\!\in\!\{1,..4\},
dy\!\in\!\{1,..3\}$, provided the arc is empty of solid/spike tiles
and the landing column has a solid floor. Cost is in game ticks
(one per row or column moved), so flat walking is cheaper than
jumping in unobstructed regions --- otherwise the descriptor
\#jumps would be trivially saturated. The solver is A* with a
horizontal-distance-to-goal heuristic; the node budget is $4000$.
Fitness rewards solvability, plan length, jump count, and moderate
pit/spike counts.

The platformer is intentionally simpler than the state-of-the-art
Mario-AI tile graph~\cite{volz2018evolving}; we keep the action
model tractable so that the descriptor families are
domain-comparable to Sokoban and so that the experiments fit a
single workstation.

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:experiments}

\textbf{Conditions:} \{\textsf{RAND}, \textsf{STR}, \textsf{PLAY},
\textsf{SKILL}, \textsf{COMMON}\}. \textbf{Seeds:} 20 per condition
per domain. \textbf{Evaluations per run:} 5,500 for Sokoban, 5,000
for the platformer, including a $250$-individual random
initialisation. \textbf{Hardware:} a single 10-core consumer
workstation; 8 parallel runs at a time.

\textbf{Metrics.} For each run we record common-archive coverage
(number of filled cells), common QD-score (sum of fitnesses across
filled cells), common max fitness, wall-clock time, and the number
of solver calls. For mechanism analysis we also persist every
individual's solvability, plan length, A* nodes expanded, descriptor
cell, and common-archive cell.

\textbf{Statistics.} For each metric we report mean$\pm$std and
percentile-bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (5000 resamples). For
pairwise condition comparisons we use the Mann--Whitney $U$ test
(two-sided) with Holm--Bonferroni adjustment~\cite{holm1979simple}
across the $\binom{5}{2}=10$ comparisons per metric per domain.
Effect sizes are Cliff's $\delta$~\cite{romano2006cliffd,
vargha2000critique}, interpreted via the standard
negligible/small/medium/large thresholds.

the platformer.

What \emph{does} replicate across domains is the gross structure
of the inter-condition comparisons. Table~\ref{tab:crossdomain}
combines the per-domain p-values for nine pre-registered
contrasts using Stouffer's Z method~\cite{stouffer1949combining}.
Eight of the nine combined p-values fall below
$10^{-3}$. The single contrast that fails to replicate is the
mechanism direction --- \emph{``\textsf{STR} retains harder
boards than \textsf{PLAY}''} --- which holds in Sokoban
($\delta\!=\!+0.74$) but reverses in the platformer
($\delta\!=\!-0.38$). This negative cross-domain result is itself
informative: it formalises the claim in
\S\ref{sec:mechanism} that the implicit-pressure mechanism's
\emph{direction}, not just its magnitude, is domain-specific.

\section{Mechanism: Implicit Hardness Selection}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\subsection{Sokoban: structural pressure inflates the failure rate}

The most striking finding is the wall-clock disparity in Sokoban.
\textsf{STR}'s purely structural descriptor $(\text{boxes},
\text{wall density})$ never asks the solver to do anything; one
might expect it to be the cheapest condition. Instead it is the
slowest, at $81.8\pm11.4$\,s versus $37.3\pm11.6$\,s for
\textsf{PLAY} ($p_\text{adj}\!<\!10^{-6}$, Cliff's $\delta=-0.99$).

Figure~\ref{fig:soko-mechanism} shows the mechanism. The grey
bars give the median $\log_{10}(\text{A* nodes expanded})$ over all
\emph{evaluated} solvable individuals; the coloured bars are over
\emph{retained} cell-winner elites. \textsf{STR}'s evaluated
distribution sits $0.78$\,decades above \textsf{PLAY}'s (median
$3.15$ vs $2.37$, i.e.\ a $\approx\!6\times$ difference in nodes
per solver call). Its retained distribution stays
$0.17$\,decades higher than \textsf{PLAY}'s (median $2.73$ vs
$2.56$); pairwise per-seed Mann--Whitney on the
per-seed-mean-of-retained gives $p_\text{adj}\!<\!10^{-3}$,
$\delta\!=\!-0.74$ (large).
The picture for the other three conditions is symmetric and small.

The reason is not that wall density is directly correlated with A*
nodes within \emph{solvable} boards (it is in fact weakly
\emph{negatively} correlated, Spearman
$\rho\!=\!-0.07$). The reason is that selection on density
preserves dense-wall elites and mutation from a dense-wall elite is
disproportionately likely to produce an
\emph{infeasible} board --- one that the solver attempts and times
out on, consuming the full $8000$-node budget. Table~\ref{tab:failure}
gives the numbers: \textsf{STR}'s mutation pipeline produces
$20.0\%$ unsolvable-but-attempted offspring versus $12.9\%$ for
\textsf{PLAY}. The extra failure work, at $8000$ nodes per attempt,
is most of the wall-clock gap.

\subsection{Platformer: the mechanism does not fire}

The platformer's A* over standing positions almost always
terminates in well under $100$ node expansions because the
horizontal-distance-to-goal heuristic is tight and the underlying
graph is sparse. The unsolvability rate is uniform across
conditions ($5{-}6\%$). Consequently there is no
condition-specific failure-work inflation:
Figure~\ref{fig:plat-mechanism} shows the per-condition
$\log_{10}(\text{nodes})$ distributions overlapping within
$\pm 0.02$, and no pairwise difference in retained-elite hardness
survives Holm correction ($p_\text{adj}\!\geq\!0.41$ for all).

Critically, this is exactly what the framework predicts. The
mechanism in \S\ref{sec:method} states that implicit hardness
selection arises when descriptor coordinates correlate with solver
hardness; if the solver problem itself does not vary much, the
descriptor cannot induce hardness variation either. The platformer
is a case where the implicit pressure is \emph{benign} --- it
reshapes \emph{what} content gets generated (PLAY is significantly
worse than COMMON in archive coverage, $p_\text{adj}\!<\!10^{-5}$)
without inflating the cost of generating it.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What descriptor should I use?}

We offer a short decision guide synthesised from the empirical
results:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{If you have a downstream metric you want diverse
        coverage on, optimise it directly.} The \textsf{COMMON}
        condition (descriptor = common projection) dominates in
        both domains, because no implicit pressure works against
        you. The cost is that the resulting archive's axes are
        whatever you decided to be diverse along --- you get no
        free additional structure.

  \item \textbf{If the descriptor coordinates push selection toward
        regions of high infeasibility, expect wall-clock to inflate.}
        ``Structural'' descriptors are not automatically cheap; in
        Sokoban they are $2.2\times$ slower than gameplay
        descriptors because mutation from a dense-wall elite is
        $1.6\times$ more likely to produce a board the solver
        cannot finish, and every such attempt consumes the full
        node budget. The relevant correlation is not
        descriptor-coordinate-vs-hardness in solvable boards but
        descriptor-coordinate-vs-\emph{infeasibility-rate}.

  \item \textbf{Gameplay descriptors are not free either.} They are
        cheap to add (the solver's plan is already paid for), but
        they may collapse into narrow corners of the common
        projection if their axes are nearly degenerate (e.g.\
        \textsf{PLAY}'s underperformance in the platformer, where
        plan-length spans a narrow $40$--$60$ range so its archive
        column is mostly empty cells that can't accept
        any individual).

  \item \textbf{Solver-effort descriptors are competitive but
        domain-sensitive.} In Sokoban \textsf{SKILL} is the
        highest-coverage condition among the indirect descriptors;
        in the platformer its coverage is statistically
        indistinguishable from \textsf{STR}'s.

  \item \textbf{Don't trust folklore.} The ordering of descriptor
        families is empirical, not theoretical. Run the protocol
        of \S\ref{sec:experiments} on a small pilot before
        committing to a descriptor for a larger study.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why descriptor learning is not a free fix}

The growing literature on learned descriptors~\cite{cully2019autonomous,
grillotti2022unsupervised, paolo2020unsupervised} side-steps the
problem we describe by making the descriptor a function of the data
rather than of designer intuition. Our findings are compatible with
that programme but do not subsume it: a learned descriptor that
happens to correlate with solver hardness will exhibit the same
implicit pressure as a hand-designed one. The learning step does
not eliminate the pressure; it shifts the choice of pressure into
the loss function of the descriptor learner.

\subsection{Limitations}

Two domains, with one solver in each, do not exhaust the space of
PCG via QD. The descriptors we compare are intentionally simple
2D axes; ``richer'' descriptors (e.g.\ Centroidal Voronoi
Tessellations \cite{vassiliades2017cvtmap}, or learned embeddings
\cite{cully2019autonomous}) may exhibit additional structure we do
not characterise here. We also assume a uniform-random parent
selection policy as in the original MAP-Elites; emitter-based
variants such as CMA-ME~\cite{fontaine2020cma} may interact with
the descriptor in ways our protocol does not measure.

%==========================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The behavioral descriptor in MAP-Elites is not a passive lens onto
the design space; it is a hidden selection pressure that can
silently double the wall-clock cost of a search the practitioner
believed cheap (Sokoban, $2.2\times$ overhead) or, with the wrong
choice, cause MAP-Elites to underperform random search outright
(platformer, where \textsf{PLAY} is significantly \emph{worse} than
\textsf{RAND}). Across both domains and nine pre-registered
contrasts, eight cross-domain consistency tests reach Stouffer
combined $p < 10^{-3}$. The single contrast that fails to
replicate is the \emph{direction} of the hardness-selection
mechanism, which is positive in Sokoban (\textsf{STR} retains
harder boards) but reverses in the platformer --- formalising the
domain-dependence of the mechanism's manifestation. We have given
a controlled experimental protocol, a mechanism analysis attributing
the wall-clock effect in Sokoban to a $1.6\times$ inflation in
mutation-induced infeasibility, and a practical decision guide
for QD-PCG practitioners. The full code, raw archives, and
per-evaluation logs are released as a replication package.
